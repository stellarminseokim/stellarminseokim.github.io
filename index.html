Stellar (Minseo) Kim

Concept-Led Designer-Researcher · Responsive Matter

I study forms that respond to a person and transform. In the beginning I was drawn to building — raising bold three-dimensional structures the way an architect raises a building. Then, in London, I encountered a structure that transformed, and something shifted: I saw that form could return, that it could move between the flat and the volumetric.

Since then, that reversibility has run through everything I make. I followed it from the hand to textiles, and on to structures that meet the body and matter that responds to it — teaching myself the language of engineering whenever the technology I needed did not yet exist.

This portfolio traces that path, from the first forms I built by hand to matter that answers a person. I have come to call the direction Sentient Aesthetics.

Guggenheim

2010·Parsons School of Design

In a collection inspired by the Guggenheim, I worked from its indefinable beauty — clean and modern, yet holding intricate detail — and let its organic layering become the layered fronts of the garments. Here I also began making material my own way, stitching rubber tubing into every seam, setting organza between layers of cashmere so a coat could be worn reversed.

Photography: Ho Chang

Undefined Shape and Refined Structure

2011·Graduation Collection · Parsons

My graduation collection turned to Metabolism, the Japanese architectural movement that sought to give buildings a sense of dynamism and movement, almost like a living body. Working in the colours of concrete, glass, stone and steel, I used the geometry of those materials to explore movement within architectural structure — no longer intuitive draping, but structure designed into the pattern itself, with stiff material fused inside the fabric to hold a form.

The aim was an extreme architectural structure that still respects the human form. What drew me was never prettiness. It was structure — and, already then, structure that moves.

Photography: Ho Chang

Cutting

2013·Central Saint Martins

In Cutting the method changed. The collection was built by cutting — pieces cut and stitched together so that the assembly itself makes the structure, rather than shaping cloth over the body. Alongside that, I developed the looks through folding. I worked the whole collection in felt, which takes a fold and keeps it.

Muse

2014·Central Saint Martins

Muse began with a person. The brief was to choose one woman and design for her, and I chose Daphne Guinness — fashionable and refined, but strong and independent with it. Someone like that would not want a garment that was only beautiful; she wears bold metal herself, and I read that as a taste for something with an edge to it. So I went to ironmongers looking for fittings strong enough to sit on a garment as detail. What had drawn me to architecture in the first place — the way many different materials and structures resolve into a single form — became the raw material of my own work.

Movement in Space

2014·Graduation Project · Central Saint Martins

In the CSM library I came across an image of a transforming metal structure in a book, and something changed. Until then I had been absorbed in raising volume; now I was seized by the idea that form could return — that it could move between the flat and the volumetric.

A flat structure rises into volume when drawn by strings, and the whole panel attaches to a plain dress with magnets. Without the panel, the dress is simple. Attached, it becomes something else entirely. The strings are not fixed: the wearer draws them until the structure takes the shape they want. The form is set by the person wearing it. Detachable, reversible, and structural: this was the first time the mechanism that now runs through all my work took shape.

Expressive

2015·ArtEZ University of the Arts

The brief was to work against my own design DNA. I listed what I disliked — check, punk, aggression, anything that felt oppressive — and then looked at each one long enough to find what was underneath it. Check has never appealed to me as a print, but taken apart, what makes a check is weaving, so I worked from the mechanism rather than the pattern. Aggression I set aside; what I kept was the boldness underneath it — a gesture released freely rather than one meant to overpower. I named the collection for that.

Freedom in Disorder

2014—2015·Seminar project

Given a pile of discarded garments and leftover cloth, we were asked to take whatever we wanted, cut it apart, and build one garment back out of it. No new material. A pinstriped suit, a nylon jacket, mesh, fleece, jersey — what I could make was decided by what was in the pile. It is the reverse of every other project here, where the material was chosen to answer the idea, and it is the only time I worked by reconstruction rather than construction.

Hazardous

2015·ArtEZ University of the Arts

The brief was to design a collection in the spirit of a house I had chosen. I chose Lanvin because its confident femininity already agreed with what I was working towards — an exaggerated silhouette carried by a woman who is independent and unhidden at once, whose beauty is displayed on her own terms rather than for anyone's gaze. I went to Paris to study the collections in the store, to watch how they were merchandised, and to try the garments on, reading how they were cut and finished. What followed was designed for that woman, not for the label. Every material in the collection was made first — prints developed from my own photographs, fabrics built as swatches — and only then taken to the stand to find silhouette and detail. Eighteen looks drawn, three made.

Joie de Vivre

2016·Graduation Collection · ArtEZ · Atelier Néerlandais, Paris

The collection began with emotion. Drawing on the kinetic drawings of Heather Hansen and the expressiveness of Pina Bausch, I sat on the floor with large sheets of paper and drew with my whole body, releasing the spirit of freedom into movement. What the drawing left behind became the material: I photographed the charcoal residue on the floor and worked those images up into prints. Others came differently — white paint dripped across black cloth, drawn the same way, with the whole body moving; a line drawn in Illustrator — and one of those I made twice over, a second version for the jersey worn beneath the outer piece, so that a single look would read as one thing. Each print was silkscreened onto its own cloth, some smooth, one where the screen itself carried the texture. The trace of the movement is what carries the emotion through the collection.

And then the same gesture became form. I sat on the floor again, drawing this time on a piece of calico as large as the charcoal sheets had been, cut parts of those lines away and draped it on the stand — but the silhouette came out long and elaborate, closer to eveningwear than to anything I wanted. So I reduced: a few simplified lines on A4 paper and on calico cut to the same size, sections cut away, the cut edges pinned at two or three points on a miniature stand until a volume stood up on its own.

Then the prints became the drawing. I printed what I had developed onto sheets of paper, slashed them where the image seemed to ask for it, and draped those in miniature — and it was there that the looks of this collection came out. Prints that never reached cloth did their work here instead.

The last piece was made the other way round, and at full size: a rectangle of cloth cut to A0, lines drawn straight onto it in pen, parts cut away, draped on the full stand. I traced that same line into a print and bonded it onto the jersey worn beneath — the one absent, the other raised. Where the miniature had pins, the garments have magnets: release them and each outer piece lies flat as a rectangle again; join them and the same volume rises.

Emotion became movement, its trace became material, and incision and connection became form. What I was after was a free spirit that came through at every layer — in the act of drawing, in the prints and the texture they were given, in the silhouette itself, and finally on the skin of the people wearing it. The collection was not made for one gender, and male dancers were part of the presentation. This reversible transformation became the central mechanism of everything I have made since.

In the portfolio I wrote that year, I set down an ambition I had no means to act on yet: to develop smart textiles — to make the material do what my hands were doing here.

Transformation in the Material

2019—·Shape-Memory Alloy · Liquid Crystal Elastomer 4D Printing

To realize transforming material through the material itself rather than through my hands, I had to understand the technology directly. I designed custom shape-memory alloy geometries, reshaping the material from its default straight form into the zigzag profiles my folding structure required; working through repeated iterations with a fabrication partner, I arrived at the exact size and form I had drawn — making the material follow the structure I intended, rather than the other way around.

Shape-memory alloy only moves one way, though — it takes the new shape and holds it — and it is metal: stiff against cloth, and awkward to join to it. Shape-memory polymer did not return on its own either. Liquid crystal elastomer was the first material that met both conditions, and printing it directly onto the textile was how to place it exactly where the structure needed it.

Working with a liquid crystal elastomer formulation developed by an engineering lab, I printed across a range of textile substrates to determine which would hold the material and actuate reliably, identifying the fabric that gave both stable adhesion and effective folding. On that substrate I developed a series of actuation patterns, testing how printed geometry governs the direction and degree of transformation, until the collaboration ended.

These material collaborations ran alongside my doctoral research rather than forming part of it. At the first lab neither route worked: printed onto textile and drawn into fibre, the material would not actuate reliably or return. At the second the actuation did work, and I kept developing by printing while fibre work ran in parallel, contributing what an engineering lab does not have on hand: what a fibre must satisfy before it can be spun, stitched and woven at all.

Since 2014 I had been making forms open and return by hand. Now it was happening in the material itself.

Structure as Sensor

2023—2026·Doctoral Research · Yonsei University

The sensor picks up the breath not through how much a material stretches, but through the change in contact area that structure creates. I designed the inner three-dimensional structure and the outer pattern together, and built every component to be detachable, modular, and adjustable in size, carrying it from concept to a wireless monitoring prototype and validating how it behaves on the body under real wearing conditions.

Now —
Responsive Matter

I am now moving toward research in which form senses a person and transforms in answer. I want to resolve which forms modulate a person's state — the moment a clouded mind wakes and attention is drawn — and to follow that principle outward from the body toward the space that surrounds it.

Sentient Aesthetics: form that senses, and the aesthetic judgment of what that sensing does to the person who meets it. Responsive matter is what I work with; this is what I work toward.
